what_i_learned: |
  Today I focused on bringing together the results of my machine learning experiments in a way that clearly communicates the story behind the data. After training and evaluating the models throughout the project, I developed a comprehensive set of publication-quality visualizations that summarize the performance of each model across every stage of the experimental framework. Creating these figures helped transform pages of performance metrics into visuals that are much easier to interpret and compare.
  
  I generated bar charts to compare the performance of the Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) models trained on the balanced dataset, as well as their performance after applying Stratified K-Fold cross-validation. I also created separate visualizations for the Douglas-Rachford optimized SVM to highlight how optimization influenced the model's predictive performance. Each visualization consistently reported evaluation metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, RMSE, MAE, and the percentage difference in RMSE. Maintaining a consistent style across all figures makes the results easier to interpret and provides a strong foundation for presenting my findings in both the research paper and the symposium presentation.
  
  As I analyzed the final results, I could clearly see how each model performed under different evaluation strategies. The Random Forest models consistently achieved high accuracy and precision, while the Douglas-Rachford optimized SVM demonstrated strong recall and ROC-AUC performance. The cross-validation results also provided greater confidence that the models were generalizing well rather than simply performing well on a single train-test split. Looking at all the models together gave me a much deeper appreciation for why multiple evaluation metrics are necessary. Accuracy alone does not tell the complete story, especially when working with imbalanced public health datasets where identifying high-risk communities is the primary objective.
  
  Beyond generating the figures, I also spent time organizing the results so they could be incorporated into the research paper and final presentation. This process helped me better understand how to communicate technical findings through both visualizations and written interpretation. Seeing the complete progression of the project, from raw data to optimized models and final comparisons, made me appreciate how much the research has evolved over the past several weeks.
  
blockers: |
  Today's challenge was deciding how to present a large amount of performance data without overwhelming the audience. With several models, balancing strategies, optimization methods, and cross-validation results, it took time to determine which visualizations best communicated the key findings while remaining clear and easy to understand.
  
reflection: |
  Today reminded me that research is not only about building accurate models but also about communicating results effectively. A well-designed visualization can explain complex findings much faster than a table full of numbers. Looking at the completed figures gave me a sense of how far this project has come, from cleaning the dataset and selecting features to optimizing models and presenting meaningful results. As I continue preparing for the symposium, I feel increasingly confident in both the quality of the research and my ability to explain it to others. Every visualization represents another piece of the story, and together they demonstrate the value of using machine learning to better understand and predict community health risks.
